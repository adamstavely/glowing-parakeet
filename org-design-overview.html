Organization design

Five portfolios, two layers, nothing in between.

A flat structure for three user-facing application portfolios, an experience design engineering portfolio, and central engineering, where coordination happens through written contracts between teams rather than through layers of management.

01

Mission, vision, and values

What the office is for, where it is going, and the commitments that hold when the trade-offs get hard. Everything structural in this document is downstream of these.

Mission
Deliver secure, human-centered software capabilities that enable mission outcomes, at the speed the mission demands.

This office exists to build, modernize, and sustain the software systems the organization depends on. We do this with engineering rigor, deliberate human-machine teaming, and a relentless focus on the people who use what we build.

Vision
A modern, AI-augmented software organization where experts lead experts, and every engineer's work is traceable to mission impact.

We are building toward an office where legacy debt is retired, delivery is continuous, AI is trustworthy and governed, and every team has the platform, capability, and culture to do the best work of their careers.

Core values
01

Mission first

Technical decisions are evaluated by their contribution to mission outcomes. Elegance without impact is not enough.

02

Experts lead experts

We hire and promote domain mastery. Every leader must be able to engage with, evaluate, and improve the work beneath them.

03

Build to last

We engineer for security, resilience, and long-term maintainability, not for speed alone. Technical debt is a mission risk.

04

Human-centered

The user is always in the room, even when they are not. We build with them, not just for them, and measure success by how well our systems serve them.

05

Trustworthy autonomy

AI augments human judgment. It does not replace it. Every system we deploy is explainable, traceable, and under meaningful human control.

06

Blameless learning

Failures are systems problems, not people problems. We acknowledge mistakes openly, learn from telemetry, and build more resilient systems as a result.

Structure Four of these show up as structural commitments rather than sentiment. Experts lead experts is why chief engineers hold architecture authority and team leads stay at roughly 60 percent hands-on. Human-centered is why experience design engineering is a portfolio rather than a service. Build to last is why modernization capacity is protected at portfolio level instead of competing with features. Blameless learning is why site reliability reports against team-owned error budgets rather than owning them. A value that cannot be traced to a structural decision is decoration.
02

The design problem

Flatness is not a structural choice. It is a downstream consequence of two things: how much cognitive load each team carries, and how much coordination has to pass through humans rather than through contracts. Hierarchy exists to absorb coordination overhead. Remove the layers without removing the overhead and it reappears immediately as meetings, escalations, and shadow leads with no title and no authority.

So the question is not how few layers we can survive with. It is what has to be made explicit so that the layers become unnecessary. Everything in this document follows from that: team sizing that caps load, service contracts that replace escalation paths, and decision rights written down before launch rather than discovered under pressure.

Four constraints shape the answer. There are three user-facing portfolios that must move at their own pace. There is a central engineering portfolio holding the platform, site reliability, and shared backend domains such as geospatial and language, which every application depends on and none of them owns. There is experience design engineering, which owns research, the design system, and the front-end craft that decides whether a reimagined application is actually better than the one it replaces. And every portfolio carries legacy applications and technical debt that have to be modernized while delivery continues.

The last constraint is the one most organization designs get wrong. Modernization is not a project that runs alongside the org chart. It is a property of how ownership is assigned.

03

What we drew from

Five established models, each with something worth taking and something worth leaving. None of them transfers whole.

Spotify · Kniberg 2012, Lee 2020

Squads, tribes, chapters, guilds

Rejected

Worth taking

  • Correctly identifies that craft development needs somewhere to live when teams are autonomous.
  • Squad-level autonomy over the backlog is the right instinct and survives in the stream-aligned model.
  • The vocabulary is widely understood, which lowers the cost of explaining any org to stakeholders.

Worth leaving

  • It was aspirational documentation of a single moment, not a working model. Kniberg attached a disclaimer to his own paper.
  • Spotify's own 2020 retrospective describes autonomy without alignment producing duplicated infrastructure and weak cross-squad accountability.
  • Chapter leads managed people whose work they could not see, which is the specific failure to avoid.
  • Tribes are a division by another name, typically 40 to 150 people. That directly violates the flatness constraint.
  • Chapters and guilds are a second network with its own leads and its own cadence. That is a layer in everything but name, and it competes with the team lead for the same attention.

Reject the structure entirely. Craft standards, practice, and technical community live with the principal engineers and the architecture council rather than in a parallel network. One horizontal structure is a compensating control; two is a matrix.

Netflix · highly aligned, loosely coupled

Context not control, full-cycle developers

Partial

Worth taking

  • The paved road is the clearest articulation of platform-as-product anywhere in the industry.
  • Full-cycle ownership, where teams operate what they build, eliminates handoff queues. That is where most delivery latency hides.
  • Genuinely flat at scale, which proves the shape is achievable.

Worth leaving

  • Entirely predicated on uniform talent density and the ability to exit people quickly. Neither condition holds here.
  • Netflix mostly deletes rather than modernizes. There is no legacy migration pattern to borrow.
  • The paved road took years and sustained investment to build. Adopting the philosophy without the platform produces drift.

Take: the paved road and full-cycle ownership. Leave: the staffing and performance philosophy.

Skelton & Pais 2019

Team Topologies

Primary framework

Worth taking

  • The four team types map onto this problem almost exactly, including the awkward parts.
  • Geospatial and language are textbook complicated-subsystem teams: specialist depth that would overload any application team asked to absorb it.
  • The three interaction modes replace hierarchy with written contracts. This is the actual mechanism that makes flat viable.
  • Cognitive load as the team sizing rule, rather than headcount or utilization.
  • The inverse Conway maneuver gives a deliberate lever on architecture through org shape.

Worth leaving

  • It is a design vocabulary, not an operating model. Silent on career ladders, performance management, funding, and acquisition.
  • Easy to fake. Renaming existing teams to the four types while changing nothing is the most common adoption failure.
  • Enabling teams are always the first capacity raided when delivery pressure arrives.

Take: the whole skeleton, with the gaps filled from the other four models.

Amazon · two-pizza teams

Single-threaded ownership

Partial

Worth taking

  • Unambiguous decision rights. Every domain has exactly one owner who can say yes without convening anyone.
  • Coordination happens through APIs rather than meetings, which is precisely what lets a flat org scale past 200.
  • Ownership is durable rather than project-scoped, which suits a modernization horizon measured in years.

Worth leaving

  • Tolerates expensive duplication as the price of autonomy. The headcount here does not support that.
  • Requires real service isolation, which the legacy estate will not provide until modernization is well advanced.
  • Service proliferation without platform discipline produces a distributed monolith, which is strictly worse than what exists now.

Take: single-threaded ownership as the decision-rights layer. Leave: the tolerance for duplication.

The default · functional matrix

Discipline-based resourcing

Rejected

Worth taking

  • Easy resource management and clear career homes by discipline.
  • Absorbs staffing volatility well, which matters in a contracted environment.
  • Familiar to contracting, staffing, and oversight, so it needs no explanation.

Worth leaving

  • Optimizes utilization rather than flow. Every engineer looks busy and nothing ships.
  • Guarantees handoffs, which produces component teams instead of value-stream teams.
  • Conway's Law then bakes those handoffs permanently into the architecture, where they outlive the org chart that created them.

Reject as the primary shape. Some functional pooling remains legitimate for programmatics and test infrastructure, and only there.

04

The shape

Team Topologies as the skeleton, the paved road as the central engineering operating model, single-threaded ownership as the decision layer, and one architecture council in place of a division tier.

A

Five portfolios, one direction of dependency

Three application portfolios own user-facing value streams end to end, including the legacy systems they are replacing. Experience design engineering owns research, the design system, and front-end craft, and works into all three. Central engineering holds the platform, site reliability, the shared backend domains, and the enabling capacity. Every connection is a service contract, not a reporting line. The outer paths are the reminder that application portfolios consume central engineering directly and do not route through design.

Central engineering is a product organization, not a service desk

This is the single move the whole design depends on. Central engineering has four customers, a published roadmap, a product manager, and adoption as its primary metric. It does not take tickets and it does not gate releases. Application teams may route around it, and if they do, that is a signal about the platform rather than a failure of compliance.

Central engineering is funded on its own line at 25 to 30 percent of total engineering, and experience design engineering at 8 to 12 percent. Below those thresholds each becomes a bottleneck: the platform gets rebuilt three times in parallel, and design becomes a queue that application teams learn to skip.

Site reliability is the one deliberate exception to pure self-service. It provides tooling, telemetry, runbooks, and operational documentation as a product, and it also holds first and second line response for the portfolio. That second duty is an operational contract rather than a platform service, and it is called out separately in section 06 because it is the place this design most obviously departs from full-cycle team ownership.

05

Team types

Four types, sized by cognitive load rather than headcount. The proportions matter more than the labels.

18teams
STREAM-ALIGNED · 3 app portfolios

Own a user-facing value stream end to end, including the legacy system it replaces. Seven to nine people. They build it, they own its behavior in production, and they are the final escalation for it. Each team owns exactly one stream and no team owns two.

3teams
PLATFORM · central engineering

Build the thinnest viable platform that makes the common path fast: build and deploy, observability, identity, data access, environment provisioning. Consumed as a self-service product, never as an approval step.

2teams
SITE RELIABILITY · central engineering

Reliability tooling, telemetry, SLO instrumentation, runbooks, and operational documentation as a product, plus first and second line response across the portfolio. Owning teams set the SLOs and take third line; SRE never owns the system itself.

3teams
COMPLICATED SUBSYSTEM · shared domains

Geospatial, language, and the other backend domains where specialist depth is real and would overload any application team asked to absorb it. Exposed as versioned services with published contracts, not as embedded specialists on loan.

3teams
EXPERIENCE DESIGN ENGINEERING · own portfolio

Two modes at once. Research, service design, and embedded design partnership are facilitating work into the application portfolios. The design system, component library, and accessibility tooling are a product consumed as a service. Design engineers write production front-end code; this is not a handoff function.

1team
ENABLING · time-boxed

Three to five senior engineers who help a stream-aligned team acquire a capability it lacks, then leave. Every engagement has an explicit end date. They never own a system and never become a permanent dependency.

Sizing Seven to nine people per team. Six teams per application portfolio, three in experience design engineering, and eight to nine in central engineering. That holds roughly 210 to 230 people across two reporting layers. Central engineering carries the widest span by some distance, which is the reason it is led by a pair rather than a single director and the first place to watch for strain. The moment a team needs a tenth person, the correct response is to split the stream, not to grow the team.
06

The contracts

This is what replaces the layers. Every pair of teams that needs each other has one of three relationships, written down, with a named owner on each side.

B

Three interaction modes, and one operational contract

Default to X-as-a-service, because it costs the least coordination. Collaboration is expensive and deliberately time-boxed: two teams working closely for a defined period to discover an interface that does not exist yet, after which the relationship converts to a service. Facilitating is how enabling and experience design engineering work into application teams. The last row is the exception: first and second line response is an operational contract rather than a service boundary, and it is the one relationship in this design where another team acts on a system it does not own.

Every contract states

  • The named owner on each side, one person, not a team alias
  • What is provided, in the consumer's language rather than the provider's
  • How it is consumed, and whether consumption requires anyone's permission
  • Expected response time for support, and what counts as an escalation
  • How breaking changes are announced and how long the old version survives
  • The review date, because contracts that never expire stop being read
  • For site reliability only: the escalation threshold at which the owning team is paged

Signals the contract has failed

  • An application team builds its own version of something the platform provides
  • A collaboration mode has been running for more than one quarter
  • An enabling engagement has no exit date, or the date has already slipped twice
  • A significant incident is closed without the owning team ever being brought in
  • Design arrives after the architecture is set, which makes it decoration rather than design
  • A domain service change requires a coordinated release across portfolios
  • Requests between teams are routed through a portfolio lead instead of directly
  • Anyone needs a meeting to find out who owns something
07

Where leadership lives

Two reporting layers vertically. Everything else that would normally require a layer is handled by a single council with no reporting authority over anyone.

C

The vertical: two layers, and that is all

Each portfolio is led by a chief engineer and a program lead operating as equals, two in a box, with the same reports and different accountabilities. Team leads report directly to that pair. There is no layer between a team lead and a portfolio lead, and no layer between a portfolio lead and the head of engineering. Spans run from three in experience design engineering to nine in central engineering, which is defensible without being comfortable at the top end.

D

The horizontal: what replaces the missing layer

The architecture council is the only compensating control for having no division tier, and deliberately the only one. Chief engineers plus principal engineers plus the platform product manager, meeting on a fixed cadence, owning every decision that crosses a portfolio boundary and every craft standard that has to hold across portfolios. It has no reporting authority over anyone, which is precisely what keeps it from becoming a layer. A second horizontal network, whatever it is called, would be a matrix.

08

Programmatics and funding

The part that decides whether the design survives contact with the budget cycle.

Two in a box, per portfolio

Chief engineer and program lead as equals with the same reports. Technical authority and programmatic authority sit side by side rather than stacked, which removes the most common reason to add a layer.

One central programmatics cell

Five to eight people covering contracts, financial management, staffing, and metrics for all four portfolios. Shared service, thin, and deliberately not replicated per portfolio.

No project managers on teams

A PM per team rebuilds the matrix by accident within two quarters. Program management sits at portfolio level and teams manage their own flow. The same rule applies to designers: they belong on the team, not assigned to it.

Flow metrics, not utilization

Lead time, deployment frequency, change failure rate, time to restore. Utilization reporting drives the wrong behavior and is the leading indicator of matrix relapse.

Persistent capacity, not projects

Fund teams as durable capacity against a value stream. Project-based funding forces reteaming at every award boundary, which destroys the ownership the model depends on.

Decision rights in writing

Single-threaded owner per domain, ADRs for anything crossing a seam. Flat without explicit decision rights is slower than hierarchy, not faster.

09

Modernization

Every portfolio carries legacy systems and debt. How ownership is assigned decides whether they ever get paid down.

Do not create legacy teams and new-build teams. It is the most common structure and the most reliably destructive one. It produces a two-class system, concentrates the highest-risk institutional knowledge in the people with the least career upside, and creates a permanent information asymmetry between the team that knows how the system actually behaves and the team designing its replacement. Every organization that has tried it has regretted it, usually around month fourteen when the first cutover fails.

The alternative is that the same stream-aligned team owns both the legacy application and its replacement, and strangles one into the other incrementally. The team that owns the old system's behavior is the team that designs the new one, which aligns the incentive to make the migration real rather than nominal.

Reimagining is where experience design engineering earns its portfolio. A strangler migration that ports the existing screens one for one produces a modern stack running a legacy experience, which is the most expensive way to change nothing. Design partnership belongs at the front of each strangler cut, before the service boundaries are set, because the boundaries and the user journey have to be decided together. Site reliability matters here too, for a duller reason: you cannot cut traffic over safely without comparable telemetry on both sides, and building that is SRE work rather than something each team invents mid-migration.

What makes it work

  • Same team owns the legacy system and its replacement, with no handoff at any point
  • Strangler fig, never a parallel rewrite with a big-bang cutover
  • Protected capacity allocation of 20 to 30 percent, set at portfolio level
  • The allocation is defended by the program lead and is not renegotiable per sprint
  • The enabling team makes the first cut with each portfolio, transfers the pattern, and exits
  • Platform capability runs one portfolio ahead of the first migration that needs it
  • Design partnership starts before service boundaries are fixed, not after
  • Comparable telemetry on the old and new paths before any traffic moves

What kills it

  • Debt work that has to compete with features for capacity, because it loses every time
  • A modernization program with its own org, schedule, and reporting chain
  • Three portfolios migrating simultaneously against an immature platform
  • Treating the legacy system as frozen, which guarantees the replacement chases a moving target
  • Measuring migration by percentage of code moved rather than percentage of traffic served
  • Porting existing screens one for one, which modernizes the stack and nothing else
  • An enabling engagement that quietly becomes permanent ownership
Sequencing Platform capability has to land roughly one portfolio ahead of the first modernization effort that depends on it. If all three portfolios start migrating before the paved road exists, each invents its own answer, and the resulting divergence is harder to unwind than the debt they set out to retire.

Culture

Structure decides what is possible. Culture decides what actually happens when the structure is inconvenient.

Culture is not a values poster. It is the accumulated weight of what an organization signals, celebrates, repeats, and decides, especially under pressure. Every organization has one. The only question is whether it was designed or whether it accumulated by default.

Designed cultures are built through consistent use of four levers: the signals leaders send through their attention and behavior, the stories that circulate about what the organization values, the rituals that create shared identity and rhythm, and the decisions that reveal what the organization actually believes when trade-offs are forced.

All four have to pull in the same direction: toward an engineering culture that is mission-driven, expert-led, human-centered, and psychologically safe. When they conflict, when a decision contradicts a stated value or a story circulates that rewards the wrong behavior, the inconsistency is louder than any value statement. Coherence across all four is a leadership responsibility, not a communications one.

Lever 01 · Signals

What leaders pay attention to

Signals are the visible behaviors, artifacts, and patterns that reveal what the organization truly values, regardless of what it says it values. Engineers watch what leaders notice, reward, correct, and ignore, and over time those observations become the real operating norms. Signals include what gets celebrated, whose work gets highlighted, which metrics leaders ask about first, and how leaders behave during incidents.

Signals to build deliberately
Engineering quality over delivery velocity. When leaders ask about code health, test coverage, and architectural decisions rather than only sprint burndown, engineers learn that quality is real and not aspirational.
Recognition of invisible work. Documentation, mentoring, code review, and operational improvement are recognized publicly alongside feature delivery.
Chief engineers visible in the work. Portfolio leads are expected to engage with the technical details of their domain. Their presence in design reviews and code discussions signals that expertise is expected at every level.
Retirement milestones treated as wins. Cutting the last traffic off a legacy system and deleting it is celebrated as a delivery achievement, not mourned as a loss of team identity.
Lever 02 · Stories

What the organization remembers and repeats

Stories are the narratives that travel through an organization, told in onboarding, all-hands, team meetings, and hallway conversations. They encode values more powerfully than any policy document because they are memorable, human, and specific. The stories an organization chooses to tell reveal what it actually believes. The stories it fails to tell, or quietly suppresses, reveal what it is afraid to confront.

Stories to tell actively
Blameless postmortem stories. Specific, named examples where honest root cause analysis led to a better system, told publicly and credited to the team rather than to the individuals who failed.
Expertise over title stories. Engineers whose technical judgment overrode a more senior decision and were right, told to reinforce that expertise earns authority here, not seniority alone.
User impact stories. Named examples of how a specific engineering decision, a design change, a latency reduction, a data enrichment, changed an analyst's or operator's experience. This is what connects code to mission.
Modernization origin stories. Why this office exists: the cost of the legacy estate, the mission gap it creates, and the commitment to close it. Told repeatedly so engineers understand what they are part of.
Lever 03 · Rituals

What the organization does repeatedly

Rituals are recurring practices that create shared identity, rhythm, and belonging. They differ from processes in that they carry symbolic weight beyond their functional purpose. A retrospective is a process; a blameless postmortem review is a ritual that encodes psychological safety. Rituals must reinforce the behaviors the culture is trying to build, and must be protected from cancellation when the calendar gets tight.

Rituals to establish and protect
Synchronized demos. All teams demo on the same day each cycle. Not a status meeting, a showcase. Cross-portfolio attendance is expected from leadership, and it builds shared awareness of what the office is delivering.
Quarterly portfolio planning. Office-wide alignment on cross-portfolio dependencies and goals. Creates a shared planning horizon and mutual accountability across portfolio lines.
Tradecraft sessions. Monthly office-wide learning where engineers share deep technical or domain expertise. Not project updates, genuine knowledge transfer. This is where craft practice lives now that there is no separate guild network.
Blameless postmortem reviews. Every significant incident produces a written postmortem and a mandatory review. The review is a ritual, not a punishment, and attendance by at least one portfolio lead signals that leadership takes learning seriously.
Lever 04 · Decisions

What the organization chooses when it is hard

Decisions are the most powerful cultural signal and the most dangerous when they contradict stated values. Every time a trade-off is forced between mission and convenience, between quality and speed, between the right hire and the available hire, the decision reveals what the organization actually believes. One bad decision made visibly and without explanation can undo months of cultural investment. Hard decisions must be explained.

Decisions that will define this office
Holding the bar on expertise in leadership. Refusing to fill a chief engineer role with a capable generalist when a domain expert is not yet ready, and saying why, establishes that experts leading experts is real.
Holding the modernization allocation under pressure. When the protected 20 to 30 percent survives a quarter with a slipping delivery date, the office learns that the modernization mandate has teeth.
Delaying a release for quality or security. When leadership chooses to delay rather than ship something that violates a standard, engineers internalize that building to last is not aspirational.
Protecting enabling and design capacity. When facilitation time is defended against being cannibalized by delivery pressure, engineers learn that capability building is a first-class investment rather than a luxury.
Coherence The four levers are only as strong as their weakest one. An office that tells expertise-over-title stories and then promotes on tenure has not sent a mixed message; it has sent a clear one, and the story is the part nobody believes. Before adding a ritual or drafting a message, check the decisions already made this quarter and confirm they say the same thing.

What will break

Flat is not free. These are the known costs and the compensating controls for each. Anything not paid for here will be paid for later at a worse exchange rate.

People development has nowhere to hide

Fifty people per portfolio with no middle layer means team leads absorb performance management, career conversations, and hiring on top of delivery. That is real work the hierarchy used to do, and removing the layer does not remove the work.

Control

Cap teams at nine. Give team leads explicit time for the management portion rather than treating it as overhead. Watch for burnout around month nine, which is when the cumulative load becomes visible.

Promotion cannot mean getting reports

In a flat org there are very few management roles to promote into. Without a real alternative, the strongest engineers conclude the ceiling is low and leave within eighteen months. This is the most common cause of death for flat structures.

Control

A functioning dual-track ladder where principal roles carry genuine authority over cross-cutting decisions, not just a higher salary band. Audit it annually by counting technical promotions against management promotions.

Flat without decision rights is slower, not faster

Removing layers removes the person who used to break ties. If nobody owns a decision, it defaults to consensus, and consensus at thirty teams is indistinguishable from paralysis.

Control

Single-threaded ownership per domain, written down before launch. ADRs for anything crossing a seam, adjudicated by the architecture council on a fixed cadence rather than on demand.

The enabling team will get raided

The moment a portfolio is behind schedule, three to five senior engineers with no permanent system assignment look exactly like available capacity. This happens in almost every organization that creates one.

Control

Either protect it structurally with its own funding line, or do not create it at all. A half-funded enabling team is worse than none, because it sets an expectation of support that is never delivered.

The platform becomes a bottleneck

Central engineering has three customers with competing priorities and no authority to say no gracefully. The failure mode is a queue, followed by application teams building their own versions of everything.

Control

Measure adoption and self-service ratio, not tickets closed. If application teams are routing around the platform, treat it as product feedback rather than a compliance problem, and fix the platform.

Site reliability creates an ownership gap

Handing first and second line to SRE is a deliberate retreat from full-cycle ownership, and the known consequence is that teams stop feeling accountable for reliability once someone else answers the page. Reliability then becomes SRE's problem, and SRE becomes an operations department with a modern name.

Control

Owning teams set their own SLOs and error budgets; SRE reports against them rather than owning them. Tier 3 escalation to the owning team is not optional. And SRE can hand a chronically unreliable system back, which is the only sanction that makes the arrangement honest.

Design gets treated as a service queue

An experience design portfolio that sits outside the delivery teams will be asked for screens after the architecture is decided. That is decoration, not design, and it produces modernized systems that users experience as unchanged.

Control

Design engineers ship production front-end code and join at the point where service boundaries are being set. If a strangler cut reaches implementation without design having shaped the journey, treat it as a process failure and say so in the review.

Situational awareness at the top degrades

At 200 people with two layers, the head of engineering cannot know what is happening by walking around, and there is no middle tier synthesizing it. Problems surface late and arrive fully formed.

Control

The architecture council plus a consistent portfolio-level flow metrics cadence. Flow-based rather than utilization-based, reviewed on the same day every month so that trends are visible rather than reconstructed. SRE telemetry helps here, since it is the one function with a view across all five portfolios.

The test Nine months in, ask two questions. When two teams disagree about an interface, how does it get resolved? If someone escalates to a portfolio lead, the layer has grown back informally and the contracts are not doing their job. And when a mission-critical system degrades at two in the morning, does the team that built it ever find out? If the answer is not reliably yes, the operational contract has become a handoff and the ownership model is already gone.
